# Research kit: testing a SaaS onboarding email writing service

## Assumptions and scope

This is a ready-to-use first research deliverable, not completed research. Assume the researcher is testing a writing service for early-stage B2B SaaS founders who oversee trial or new-user onboarding. No product, segment, interview answers or verified buying intent was provided. Narrow the segment before recruiting; do not assume email engagement is poor or that writing is the cause. The two supplied requests are from the same repository and may represent one initiative; they are not evidence of two buyers.

Working hypothesis: some founders have a recurring onboarding problem that email writing could address and have a practical way to pay for outside help. Alternative explanations include weak product fit, broken email delivery, unclear in-product guidance, low signup quality or no meaningful problem.

This Markdown contains the full guide and an empty spreadsheet-ready structure. It is not a DOCX or XLSX. For a customer order, transfer the guide into DOCX and the tracker into XLSX; the pilot service delivers those editable formats. No research execution or onboarding email writing is included in this kit.

## Run the research

1. Choose one segment, for example founder-led B2B SaaS with a self-serve trial. Recruit 10 people responsible for new-user onboarding, including those who report no email problems. Avoid selecting only dissatisfied founders.
2. Find relevant public founder discussions and communities that permit research requests. Use one initial message per person through an allowed channel. Do not scrape addresses or message the same person across channels. Log invitations separately from interviews so response rate is not mistaken for demand.
3. Send outreach variant 1 for a direct invitation, 2 for a permitted public post, or 3 when responding to an existing discussion. Follow up after 4–5 days, then once more after another 5–7 days; stop after a decline or the second unanswered follow-up.
4. Run a 20–25 minute conversation: consent and context (2 minutes), questions 1–8 (13 minutes), questions 9–12 (8 minutes), recap (2 minutes). Invite written answers if easier. Skip irrelevant questions and accept “I don't know.” Do not pitch the service before exploring past behavior.
5. Record one anonymized participant per tracker row. Ask before recording audio; text notes are sufficient. Do not request user-level analytics, private customer lists, credentials or identifiable end-user data. Keep any necessary contact details separate and delete them when follow-up is complete. Set a notes retention period with participants, such as 30 days after synthesis.

Opening script: “I'm exploring how early-stage SaaS teams handle new-user onboarding and whether outside help would be useful. I'm testing an idea, not assuming you have a problem. There are no right answers. Is it okay if I take anonymized notes? You can skip anything or stop at any time. Please avoid sharing customer details or confidential numbers.”

## Twelve neutral interview questions

1. What is your role in onboarding new users, and what does that process currently look like? Probe: What counts as a user's first useful outcome?
2. Thinking of the most recent new-user cohort you reviewed, what happened between signup and that first useful outcome? Probe: What did you observe directly, and what is still unknown?
3. Where, if anywhere, did those users get stuck? Probe: Can you describe a recent example without identifying the user?
4. What role, if any, do emails play in that process today? Probe: Who writes them, and what triggers them?
5. How do you judge whether onboarding is working? Probe: Which measures do you trust, and what do they leave out? If opens are mentioned, ask whether privacy-related opens or tracking limitations affect interpretation.
6. When did you last change the onboarding process or emails, and what prompted that change? Probe: What changed afterward, and what other factors might explain it? If never, ask why the current process has stayed in place.
7. What impact, if any, have onboarding difficulties had on your team's work or business? Probe: Can you give a recent example or a rough estimate, and how did you arrive at it?
8. What have you tried to address those difficulties, including doing nothing, and how has that worked? Probe: What time, tools, internal work or outside help did it take?
9. Tell me about the last time you paid for help with onboarding, email writing or a comparable marketing task, if ever. Probe: What did you buy, what did it cost if you can share, and why did you choose it? Record the currency and scope; comparable spending is not proof of demand for this service.
10. How do you decide whether an onboarding improvement gets attention compared with other priorities? Probe: Who decides, and is there a real deadline or budget process?
11. If you were considering outside help for onboarding emails, what would need to be true for you to consider paying, and what would rule it out? Probe: What scope, evidence or spending limit would apply, if any? Label every hypothetical answer as hypothetical; do not introduce a price range first.
12. What, if anything, do you intend to do next about onboarding? Probe: Is there a concrete next step or date, and would any further discussion be useful? “Nothing” is a useful answer; do not push for commitment.

Closing script: “I heard [brief factual recap]. What have I misunderstood? Thank you. May I contact you once to clarify these notes? It's fine to say no.”

## Three recruitment messages

Replace bracketed fields with true details before using these research invitations. These messages recruit the researcher's interview participants; they are distinct from the replies promoting the free kit.

### 1. Direct invitation

Hi [name], I'm researching how founders of [segment] SaaS products help new users get started. Do you oversee onboarding at [product]? If so, would you be open to a 20-minute conversation about your current process, what works and what doesn't? I'm exploring a possible onboarding email writing service, but this conversation is research and has no purchase obligation. Written answers are also welcome. No customer data is needed, and no is completely fine.

### 2. Permitted community post

I'm looking to learn from founders who handle onboarding for [segment] SaaS products, including people who are happy with their current process. I'm exploring a possible onboarding email writing service and want to understand recent experiences before building it. Would anyone be open to a 20-minute research conversation or written questions? No purchase is expected and no private customer information is needed. If interested, reply and I'll arrange a suitable format.

### 3. Relevant discussion reply

Your point about [specific detail actually in the discussion] relates to research I'm doing on new-user onboarding at [segment] SaaS products. What happened the last time you tried to address it? If you'd like to share more, I'd welcome a 20-minute conversation or written answers about your process and what you've already tried. I'm testing an onboarding email writing service idea; there's no purchase obligation and no need to share customer data.

## Two follow-ups

### 1. After 4–5 days without a response

Hi [name], following up once on my onboarding research invitation. A short written answer would also help: what did you last change in new-user onboarding, and why? If this isn't relevant or you prefer not to take part, no problem.

### 2. Final follow-up, 5–7 days later

Hi [name], closing the loop on my onboarding research invitation. If you'd still like to participate, let me know whether written questions or a short conversation suits you. Otherwise I'll leave it here and won't follow up again. Thank you.

Column guidance: questions 1–8 map to the context, observation, problem, email, measures, change, impact and current-solutions columns. Question 9 maps to past spending, question 10 to priority, question 11 to hypothetical payment conditions and question 12 to next steps. Add interview date, recruitment source and consent before taking notes. Use “not asked,” “declined,” “unknown” or “none reported” when applicable; a blank is not a no. Keep quotes distinct from interpretation. Record all currencies as stated without silently converting them.

Problem_evidence values: unknown / none reported / general complaint / recent concrete example / repeated concrete examples. These describe evidence quality, not a numerical pain score.

Payment_evidence values: unknown / no budget stated / hypothetical interest only / relevant past spending / current approved budget reported. Record supporting context; a reported budget is not payment or a commitment to buy this offer. A polite reply or permission to follow up is not willingness to pay.

Optional second sheet, Recruitment: use columns Participant_ID, Source, Invitation_date, Variant, Followup_1_date, Followup_2_date, Status, Stop_contact. Use statuses invited / declined / no response / scheduled / completed. Do not put private contact details into a shared workbook.

## Synthesis worksheet and decision rule

After the interviews, fill this in without inventing missing answers:

- Segment and recruitment method: ____
- Invited: ____; completed: ____; declined: ____; no response: ____
- Concrete recent onboarding problem: ____ / ____ completed; supporting IDs: ____
- Similar recurring problem plausibly addressable by email writing: ____; IDs and alternative explanations: ____
- Current workarounds and their costs, with currencies and scopes: ____
- Relevant past spending: ____; reported current budgets: ____; hypothetical interest only: ____
- Strongest counterevidence, including satisfied founders and non-email causes: ____
- Proposed service scope to test next: ____
- Decision, evidence and biggest uncertainty: ____

Suggested exploratory gate, chosen before interviews: consider a narrowly scoped offer test if at least 4 of 10 completed interviews describe a similar recent problem plausibly addressable by email writing and at least 2 describe relevant past spending or a current budget decision. These are working thresholds, not statistical validation. If fewer than 10 interviews finish, report the actual denominator and treat the gate as incomplete. If needs differ, narrow the segment and research again. If problems are absent or mostly outside email, pause or change the hypothesis.
