# Customer discovery field kit

This is a usable text edition: scripts, recording tables, coding rules, and synthesis instructions. No interviews have been conducted. All example answers below are fictional.

## Assumptions and boundaries

Only the supplied excerpts were available; the linked issues and their surrounding documents were not accessed. Product behavior, geography, participant access, and commercial terms are unknown.

- Marketplace track: assume a marketplace involving merchants, buyers, couriers/carriers, and providers of managed hosting. Four separate scripts are supplied here to address the four roles explicitly requested. This reference set is an exception to the standard pilot package of three scripts/up to three segments. A standard order must select three roles; do not silently combine the fourth.
- Reading track: assume a tool for handling newsletter or reading overload, with self-hosting developers and newsletter-overloaded professionals/knowledge workers as candidate segments. “PRODUCT_STRATEGY §2 and Appendix B” were not supplied. The scripts below are a standalone substitute, not a reproduction or assertion of compliance. Before research that requires those sections, obtain them and reconcile wording and coverage.
- Each script lasts 20 minutes. Use the reading track for the requested ten interviews: five self-hosting developers and five newsletter-overloaded professionals. The marketplace track is a separate study; do not pool its results with the reading track.
- The client recruits participants and conducts interviews. These are research prompts, not legal consent or privacy documents. Use the organization’s applicable process before collecting personal data.

## Interviewer instructions

Recruit people with recent relevant experience, including people satisfied with their current approach. Do not recruit only enthusiasts for your proposed solution. For the reading study, assign people who fit both segments to one primary segment before interviewing and count each person once.

Use anonymous participant IDs. Keep scheduling details outside the research table. Take minimal notes; omit names of customers, employers, private messages, credentials, and identifying anecdotes. Agree who can access notes and when they will be deleted before starting. Do not record by default.

Say at the start:

“I’m learning how you handle this today; this is not a sales call. We have 20 minutes. You can skip a question or stop at any time. May I take brief notes without your name for this research? Please avoid sharing confidential or identifying details.”

If the participant declines notes, ask whether a conversation without retained notes is acceptable; otherwise stop. Recording requires a separate explicit agreement and the appropriate process. Do not imply this opening replaces legal requirements.

Ask one question at a time. Use neutral follow-ups: “What happened next?”, “Can you give a recent example?”, “What made that difficult?”, “What worked well?” Let silence do some work. Never supply a pain for the participant to agree with.

Keep the schedule. Optional probes are alternatives, not a checklist to rush through. If time runs out, mark unanswered fields “not asked”; never infer an answer. Do not pitch features during the problem interview.

## Track A — marketplace scripts

### A1. Merchant — 20 minutes

| Time | Main prompt | Optional probe |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Read the opening and ask: What do you sell, and what is your role in a typical sale? | Which channels do you use now? |
| 2–6 | Walk me through your most recent completed order, from listing to payment and delivery. | Where did you switch tools or contact someone? |
| 6–10 | What, if anything, was difficult in that order? | What happened as a result? If nothing was difficult, what made it work? |
| 10–14 | How do you handle that situation today? | What have you tried or stopped using? What did you last pay in fees, software, or staff time, and for what period? |
| 14–18 | Tell me about the last time you chose or changed a tool for selling. | Who decided and paid? What triggered the decision? What evidence would you need before paying for a different approach? “No reason to change” is a valid answer. |
| 18–20 | Of the difficulties you mentioned, which would you address first, if any? | Summarize their answer, ask what you misunderstood, and thank them. |

### A2. Buyer — 20 minutes

| Time | Main prompt | Optional probe |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Read the opening. What was the last item you bought through a marketplace? | Roughly when was that? |
| 2–6 | Walk me through finding it, deciding, paying, and receiving it. | What information did you use to make your decision? |
| 6–10 | What worked well, and what, if anything, got in your way? | Did you abandon or delay any step? What happened next? |
| 10–14 | How did you handle that, and what other options did you consider? | What did you actually spend on shipping, protection, subscriptions, or an alternative? Separate the item price from service costs. |
| 14–18 | Think of your last choice between a free and paid buying-related service. How did you decide? | If you have never made that choice, note it. Under what circumstances, if any, would you consider a paid service? Who pays? |
| 18–20 | Which part of that experience would you change first, if any? | Reflect back their account and invite corrections. |

### A3. Courier or carrier — 20 minutes

| Time | Main prompt | Optional probe |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Read the opening. What is your role in accepting, dispatching, or delivering parcels? | Are you an independent courier, dispatcher, or carrier decision-maker? |
| 2–6 | Walk me through the last marketplace shipment you handled. | How did shipment details, status updates, and payment reach you? |
| 6–10 | Where, if anywhere, did that shipment require extra work? | What was the consequence in time, failed attempts, or cost? Distinguish estimates from tracked figures. |
| 10–14 | How do you manage those situations now? | Which tools or manual steps do you use? What was the last actual spend on a relevant tool or integration? |
| 14–18 | How was your current delivery-management setup selected? | Who owns the budget? What would have to be true to consider switching or paying for another option? What would block it? |
| 18–20 | What is the most important unresolved issue from your account, if any? | Confirm the summary. Do not treat an employee’s interest as purchasing authority. |

### A4. Managed-hosting provider — 20 minutes

Assume “managed-hosting” means a provider/operator hosting marketplace instances for others. If it instead means a customer buying hosting, revise this role before recruiting.

| Time | Main prompt | Optional probe |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Read the opening. What do you operate, and for whom? | Which deployment or support responsibilities belong to you? |
| 2–6 | Walk me through the last marketplace deployment, update, or support incident. | What triggered it, and how did you know it was complete? |
| 6–10 | What, if anything, was difficult or uncertain? | What happened to operator time, service availability, or the customer? Avoid sensitive system details. |
| 10–14 | What tools and processes do you use today? | What have you replaced? What did you last spend on infrastructure, tooling, or support? Keep infrastructure and software costs separate. |
| 14–18 | Describe the last purchase decision for an operations tool. | Who approved it? What technical or commercial requirements ruled options out? What evidence would justify a new paid tool? |
| 18–20 | Which issue would you tackle next, if any? | Read back the key point and ask what is missing. |

## Track B — reading and newsletter study

### B1. Self-hosting developer — 20 minutes

| Time | Main prompt | Optional probe |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Read the opening. What have you self-hosted recently, and how does reading or newsletters fit into your work? | Do not assume they self-host a reading tool. |
| 2–6 | Walk me through the last time you collected, read, or retrieved something useful from newsletters or saved reading. | What triggered the task? What tools and steps did you use? |
| 6–10 | What was easy, and what, if anything, was difficult? | When did this last happen before that? What consequence did it have? |
| 10–14 | What have you tried to handle this? | What worked, failed, or was abandoned? What did you last spend on software or hosting, and how much maintenance time did it take? |
| 14–18 | Tell me about the last time you decided whether to pay for a reading or information-management tool. | Who paid? What mattered? If no purchase occurred, why? Under what conditions, if any, would a new option deserve a paid trial? |
| 18–20 | Which problem you described matters most, if any? | Confirm the summary; ask whether anything important was missed. |

### B2. Newsletter-overloaded professional / knowledge worker — 20 minutes

| Time | Main prompt | Optional probe |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Read the opening. What role does newsletter reading play in your work or personal projects? | What makes you describe it as overloaded? |
| 2–6 | Walk me through the last time you processed a batch of newsletters or tried to find something you had read. | What were you trying to get done? What did you actually do? |
| 6–10 | What, if anything, got in the way? | What did you miss, postpone, or redo? What worked well despite the volume? |
| 10–14 | How do you manage it now, and what alternatives have you tried? | What did you last pay for relevant tools or services? Note time costs separately from money. |
| 14–18 | Describe your last decision to pay—or not pay—for help with reading or information management. | Was it your budget or an employer’s? What would justify considering another option? What would stop you? |
| 18–20 | Which issue would you solve first, if any? | Reflect their answer back without naming your proposed product as the solution. |

### Optional willingness-to-pay probe

Use within minutes 14–18 only after discussing actual behavior. Ask: “Is there an existing budget for this kind of task? If so, what does it cover and over what period?” If there is no past purchase or budget, record that; do not turn it into a guessed price.

If a participant volunteers a price, ask what capabilities, billing period, currency, and approval conditions they have in mind. Mark it **hypothetical**, not revenue or purchase intent. Hosting spend is not automatically a software budget. Interest in a free trial is not willingness to pay.

### Tab 3: Pain codebook

These are starting categories, not predicted findings. Apply only where supported; add codes for unexpected issues.

| Code | Include when evidence shows | Do not infer from |
|---|---|---|
| FIND | Trouble locating a previously seen item | Simply saving many items |
| VOLUME | Incoming volume interferes with a stated task | Newsletter subscription count alone |
| HANDOFF | Moving information between tools/people creates work or errors | Use of multiple tools alone |
| RELIABILITY | A failure or uncertain behavior disrupts a task | A generic preference for reliability |
| SETUP | Deployment, configuration, or maintenance causes difficulty | Being a developer |
| COST | Actual cost or a budget limit affects a decision | Asking whether something is free |
| TRUST | A concrete trust/privacy concern changes behavior | An analyst’s assumed concern |
| OTHER | A supported pain not covered above; describe it | Vague positive or negative sentiment |
| NONE | Participant explicitly reports no relevant difficulty | Missing answer |

Record severity descriptively: inconvenience, delayed task, failed task, or unknown, with the participant’s consequence. Do not invent numeric scores or treat interview emphasis as frequency.

| Segment | Job/theme | Supporting participant IDs | Distinct people mentioning / people asked | Contrary or satisfied cases | Current tools/workarounds | Past spending evidence | Hypothetical interest separately | Interpretation + uncertainty | Next test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

After each interview, spend 5–10 minutes cleaning notes and linking every summary to evidence. Preserve corrections and distinguish quotes from paraphrases.

After the ten interviews:

1. Confirm the achieved sample: completed and partial counts per segment, recruitment source, and missing topics. Report any shortfall from five per segment.
2. Group jobs and pains without forcing every answer into the initial codebook. Check contradictory evidence and people content with their tools.
3. Count distinct participants per theme, never quote count. Use the number actually asked about the topic as denominator. Report segment counts such as “3 of 5 interviewed developers”; do not extrapolate to a population.
4. List tools and workarounds, keeping reported time, software spending, hosting costs, and hypothetical budgets separate.
5. Select one next test for each important uncertainty. A frequent pain with no spending evidence calls for a purchasing test, not a claim of validated demand.
6. Write the decision memo below. Preserve “insufficient evidence” as an acceptable outcome.

## Completed fictional example — demonstration only

Participant EX-D (not D01–D05), self-hosting developer, completed example interview; date not applicable; notes permission assumed for this invented scenario.

- Job: retrieve a useful newsletter item while writing a weekly technical digest.
- Episode: last Friday, searched an inbox and bookmarks for an article seen earlier that week.
- Tools: email search, browser bookmarks, and a self-hosted feed reader.
- Quote (invented): “I searched for about fifteen minutes, then left that reference out.”
- Main pain: FIND. Consequence: delayed writing and omitted reference. Recurrence: participant estimates twice in the last month; not independently verified.
- Budget owner: participant. WTP evidence: hypothetical only for a new reading tool.
- Quote (invented): “I might try something at five euros a month if I could export everything, but I would need to test it.”
- Analyst interpretation: retrieval may be worth exploring; whether the issue recurs across the segment and merits paid software remains unknown.
- Open question: would the participant adopt a different retrieval workflow when compared with improving their existing search?
